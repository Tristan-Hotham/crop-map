Wheat on the steppes, rice through monsoon Asia, soya beans across the Americas, cocoa in West Africa. I mapped the leading crop on every 10 km square of the world's farmland, and set it beside sixty years of change in how much land we farm.

Most maps of farming colour a whole country one shade. But a country is not one farm. The Punjab grows wheat in winter and rice in summer, the American Midwest is a patchwork of maize and soya beans, and a drive of a few hundred kilometres across Spain takes you from olive groves to wheat to grazing land. I wanted to see what the world grows at the finest scale that open data allow.

Two sources make it possible. CROPGRIDS, published in 2024, estimates the area of 173 crops on a grid of 0.05 degrees, about 5.6 km, for around 2020. I used its {{meta.n_crops|int}} biggest and best-known crops, which between them cover {{fao.share34_2020|pc}} of the world's harvested area. FAOSTAT, the statistics of the UN's Food and Agriculture Organization, gives every country's cropland, pasture and crops every year since 1961.

Most farmland is grass

The first surprise is how little of the farmed world is under crops. FAO counts {{land.agri_2023|mha}} million hectares of farmland, {{land.agri_share|pc}} of the world's land, and two thirds of it is pasture and grazing. Cropland covers {{land.crop_2023|mha}} million hectares, {{land.crop_share|pc}} of the land. Cropland has grown by {{land.crop_chg_pct|pc}} since 1961 and has never been larger, but pasture peaked in {{land.past_peak_year|raw}}, so the world's total farmland is now {{land.agri_since_peak_mha|int}} million hectares smaller than at its peak.

Cropland has also moved. The biggest gains since the early 1960s were in Brazil (+{{land.top_gain_kha.0.v|kmha}} million hectares), Argentina (+{{land.top_gain_kha.1.v|kmha}}), Indonesia (+{{land.top_gain_kha.2.v|kmha}}) and China (+{{land.top_gain_kha.3.v|kmha}}). The biggest loss was in the United States, {{land.top_loss_kha.0.v|kmhaabs}} million hectares fewer, followed by Italy, Poland, Spain and France. The countries of the former Soviet Union farm {{land.ussr.loss_since_1990_mha|f1}} million hectares less cropland than the USSR did in its last years.

Four crops, half the harvest

Wheat, maize, rice and soya beans cover {{crops.top4_share_2024|pc}} of the world's harvested land. The growth since 1961 has been driven by oil and animal feed: soya beans, oil palm and maize account for {{crops.soy_palm_maize_gain_share|pc}} of the extra {{crops.world_gain_mha|int}} million hectares. Oil palm has grown eightfold and soya beans sixfold, while the world now harvests {{crops.by_k.rye.chg_pct|pcabs}} less rye and {{crops.by_k.oats.chg_pct|pcabs}} less oats than in 1961.

And the belts are just where you would expect them. {{belts.rice_asia|pc}} of the world's rice is harvested in South, Southeast and East Asia, {{belts.soy_americas|pc}} of its soya beans in the Americas, {{belts.palm_idn_mys|pc}} of its oil palm in Indonesia and Malaysia, and {{belts.cocoa_wafrica|pc}} of its cocoa in West Africa.

Half of the world's harvested area of these crops lies on just {{grid.half_on_land_pct|f1}}% of its land.

That concentration is striking: the busiest farmland, from the Ganges plain to the Corn Belt, carries most of the harvest, and nine tenths of it lies on {{grid.ninety_on_land_pct|pc}} of the land.

Every country's speciality

Wheat is the biggest crop in {{top_crop.by.Wheat|int}} countries, maize in {{top_crop.by.Maize|int}} and rice in {{top_crop.by.Rice|int}}. More fun is the speciality: the crop a country grows far more of than the world does, relative to its farmland. Saudi Arabia gives {{spec.SAU.share|pc}} of its harvested land to dates, {{spec.SAU.lq|int}} times the world share. Madagascar's speciality is vanilla, New Zealand's kiwi fruit, Bolivia's quinoa and Turkey's hazelnuts. And the United Kingdom's? Field beans.

Closer to home

The UK is mostly grass: pasture covers {{uk.lu.past_pct|pc}} of its land and cropland {{uk.lu.crop_pct|pc}}. On the arable land, wheat, barley and rapeseed fill {{uk.wbr_share_2224|pc}} of the harvested area. Wheat dominates the east and south, barley the north and Scotland, and potatoes and sugar beet cluster in East Anglia and the Fens. Sixty years have reshuffled the fields: oats fell from {{uk.oats.1961|int}} hectares to {{uk.oats.2024|int}}, while rapeseed, which barely existed in 1961, peaked at {{uk.rapeseed.peak|int}} hectares in {{uk.rapeseed.peak_year|raw}}.

How detailed can we get?

Honestly, about 10 km for a single recent year, and the country for anything over time. CROPGRIDS is a model stitched together from 29 earlier maps and statistics, and where an input was coarse it is spread across finer cells. It also makes mistakes: it puts {{grid.eu_maize.GBR|int}} hectares of maize in the UK, where FAOSTAT records none (it is probably silage maize), so on the map maize leads in much of western Britain and Ireland. For change since 1961 there is only the country and the year.

How I did this

I summed the CROPGRIDS harvested area of {{meta.n_crops|int}} crops into 0.1 degree squares and found the leading crop, the runner-up and a diversity score in each. For countries I used FAOSTAT's bulk land use and crop downloads, removing regional totals and counting China once, and averaged crops over 2022 to 2024. Specialities use a location quotient, the crop's share of a country's harvested land divided by its share of the world's. This is a map of area, not of production or value, and it leaves out vegetables, most fruit and grass. The paper sets out every step and caveat.
